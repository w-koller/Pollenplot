Honey bees live on the honey stored in their hive through winter and whenever few flowers are giving nectar. Please check the stores often at these times, because a colony starves once its honey is gone, as Agriculture Victoria explains. Agriculture Victoria gives three ways to keep a colony from starving.

- Move the bees to an area where plants are giving nectar.
- Feed the bees white table sugar.
- Feed them syrup made with white sugar.

Colonies can be kept alive for long periods on white sugar.

## Whether to feed

- Extension AUS describes beekeepers feeding sugar syrup or pollen in the cooler months, to help colonies through winter and build up their numbers, and calls feeding important to keep a colony from starving.
- Extension AUS's spring advice is to look at the stores left from winter and decide whether the colony needs feeding.
- Agriculture Victoria's advice is to boost the stores of a colony that is short for winter with enough syrup, before the cold weather of autumn sets in, so the bees can fully process it.
- In the trials Extension AUS describes, the results were mixed and depended on how much natural forage there was. Supplements can cost more than the extra honey they bring.
- Please feed responsibly and follow the Biosecurity Code of Practice.

## Where and when to feed

- Please put sugar syrup and dry sugar inside the hive, never out in the open. Feed left in the open also feeds the managed and feral colonies nearby, wastes money, and can set off robbing and spread bee diseases.
- Please feed towards evening, which lowers the risk of bees robbing the hives you have fed.

## Honey and pollen as feed

- Please feed bees honey only from your own hives that are free of disease. Honey can carry the spores of American foulbrood, so honey from an unknown source, such as a supermarket or even another beekeeper, can infect your hives, as Agriculture Victoria explains.
- Please never feed bees honey or pollen from other colonies unless it has been irradiated, because honey and pollen from a hive with American foulbrood carry its spores, as BeeAware explains.
- Please put any honey you feed inside the hive. Putting honey in the open outside a hive is illegal in Victoria, under the Livestock Disease Control Act 1994.
- The Code asks for feed, like bees and equipment, to come from a reliable source.

## Sugar syrup

Beekeepers' views differ on how strong syrup should be, as Agriculture Victoria notes.

- Syrup of 1 part sugar to 1 part water by weight, known as 1:1, is generally used to add to the honey stores, to get a colony rearing brood, and to have the bees draw out foundation, particularly in spring.
- A dense syrup of 2 parts sugar to 1 part water, known as 2:1, is used as food when the honey stores are low.
- The sugar and water can be measured by weight or by volume, because the strength need not be exact.

Agriculture Victoria makes syrup like this.

1. Heat the water in a container big enough to hold both the water and the sugar.
2. Take the container off the heat as soon as the water gently boils.
3. Pour in the sugar and stir until the crystals have dissolved.
4. Let the syrup cool to room temperature before you feed it to the bees.

> Please never boil the syrup once the sugar is in. The sugar can caramelise, which makes it partly indigestible and toxic to bees.

## Feeders

Agriculture Victoria describes four feeders that hold syrup inside the hive.

- **Container feeder.** Fill a clean jar, or a tin with a push-down lid, with syrup, and drill or punch 6 to 8 very small holes in the lid. Taking out the cardboard insert that jar lids often have is a good idea. Lay two wooden risers 12 mm high across the top bars of the top box, and stand the container upside down on them. Put an empty super on the hive around the feeder, then the lid. The risers leave a bee space between the top bars and the holes.
- **Plastic bag feeder.** Fill a freezer bag about half full of syrup, gently squeeze out all the air and tie the neck with an elastic band. Lay the bag on the top bars of the top box, under the lid, and punch about 6 to 8 small holes in its upper surface with a thin nail, for the bees to suck the syrup through. Please never put holes in its underside, because syrup can leak out faster than the bees can take it, which wastes it outside the hive and draws robber bees. The bees need a bee space between the bag and the lid, and a wooden riser the size of the hive can raise the lid when it is needed.
- **Shallow tray feeder.** Pour syrup into a shallow tray, such as an aluminium foil tray, under the lid. Put grass straw or wood straw in the syrup, so the bees can reach it without falling in and drowning. Please never use straw or other floating material that has been treated with chemicals or has touched them, because it may harm the bees. Please keep the hive on level ground so syrup is not lost, and use a riser if the tray is not shallow.
- **Frame feeder.** A frame or division board feeder is a container the size of a full-depth Langstroth frame, open at the top, which sits in the super as a frame does. It needs flotation material, so the bees can reach the syrup without drowning.

## How much and how often

- Bees take syrup from a feeder, reduce its water and store it in the comb as if it were honey. A medium to strong colony usually empties a feeder in a matter of days, whatever kind of feeder it is.
- The first feed for a colony with almost no honey stored and no nectar coming in depends largely on how much brood it has and how big it is, and to some degree on the size of the container.
- Please try about 1 to 3 L first, then check the combs often to see how much has been stored, and let that guide how much and how often you feed. It is safer to over-feed a colony than to skimp and risk the colony dying.
- Feeding can stop once nectar becomes available.
- Properly ripened syrup holds about 18% moisture. Syrup that is not ripened properly ferments and harms the bees.

## Dry sugar

- Medium to strong colonies can be fed dry white table sugar on hive mats or in-trays under the lid. The bees need water to dissolve the crystals, which they bring from outside the hive or take from condensation inside it.
- Some beekeepers wet the sugar so it does not set solid, which makes a partial syrup.
- Please don't feed dry sugar to a weak colony, because it may not be able to gather enough water.
- Dry sugar works best in autumn and spring, when the air is fairly humid. Bees find it hard to dissolve the crystals in a hot, dry summer.
- Please give a starving colony syrup before dry sugar, so the bees have food straight away without having to dissolve crystals.
- Bees generally leave dry sugar alone while they can collect enough nectar. It stays in the hive or the bees drop it outside the entrance, and only a little may be turned to liquid and stored in the cells.

## Honey from fed colonies

- Sugar left in the combs must not be extracted with the next honey crop. It contaminates the honey, which then does not meet the legal standard for honey, Standard 2.8.2 of the Food Standards Code.
- Extension AUS's advice is not to extract honey made from feeding. Frames of it do not meet the legal standard, and extracting honey made from sugar syrup can make that batch appear to be adulterated with C4 sugars.
- Ideally the bees have eaten all the sugar given to the hive by the time it is on a honey flow, though that is not always possible. While the brood nest grows, the bees may move sugar stored in its combs to the honey super.
