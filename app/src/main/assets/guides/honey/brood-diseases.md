Healthy brood is pearly white. Brood that has turned yellow or brown, a patchy brood pattern, and cappings that are sunken, greasy or holed are reasons to look closely at a hive.

## American foulbrood

American foulbrood (AFB) is caused by the spore-forming bacterium *Paenibacillus larvae*. It is the most significant bee disease already in Australia.

- Infected larvae die after their cells are capped, and turn from pearly white to dark brown.
- Cappings may look sunken, darker or greasy, and bees trying to remove the dead brood may make holes in them.
- The dead larva becomes a light to dark brown semi-liquid mass. If it can be drawn out into a dark brown ropy thread 3 to 5 cm long, the hive could have AFB.
- The remains dry into a tough but brittle scale that is hard to remove from the cell.

AFB spreads on equipment from infected hives, when bees rob infected hives, and in their honey and pollen, which carry its spores. Please never feed bees honey or pollen from other colonies unless it has been irradiated.

> AFB must be reported in every state and territory. If you think a hive has AFB, please contact your state or territory's department of agriculture. Antibiotics are not lawful for controlling AFB in Australia.

## European foulbrood

European foulbrood (EFB) is caused by the bacterium *Melissococcus plutonius*. It is in every state and territory except Western Australia and the Northern Territory.

- Most infected larvae die before their cells are capped.
- Dead larvae turn from pearly white to yellow, then brown, and may lie coiled or twisted in the cell.
- The brood pattern is patchy, and there may be a sour smell, like ammonia.

EFB is more likely when a colony is under stress, and it stays infectious for several years in honey, wax and equipment.

> EFB must be reported in every state and territory except Queensland. If you think a hive has EFB, please contact your state or territory's department of agriculture.

## Chalkbrood

Chalkbrood is caused by the fungus *Ascosphaera apis*. It is most common in spring, when the weather is cooler.

- Hard, shrunken, chalk-like mummies, white to grey-black, lie in the brood cells and in and around the hive entrance.
- The brood pattern is scattered, and the cappings of dead larvae may have small holes.

Please replace diseased comb with new frames and foundation, clear the mummies from the bottom board and the entrance, and keep the hive somewhere dry and airy with the sun on its entrance. Chemical treatments are best avoided, because they can contaminate the honey and the bees.

## Sacbrood

Sacbrood is caused by a virus. It can become severe when a colony is short of nectar or pollen.

- Dead larvae lie stretched out on their backs, with their heads raised towards the top of the cell.
- They turn from pearly white to yellowish, then brown, and their skin becomes a tough sac filled with fluid.
- Cappings scattered through the brood may be sunken, discoloured or holed, much as they are with AFB.

If more than 5% of the brood has sacbrood, requeening is worth considering. Infected combs can be melted down or stored for at least two months.

## For every brood disease

- Please replace brood combs every three to four years, because old comb can hold disease.
- Please treat equipment from a diseased hive as infected, and clean your gear before you inspect another apiary.
